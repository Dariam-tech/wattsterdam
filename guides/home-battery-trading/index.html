Can a home battery make you money by trading? Not yet

No. A home battery saves money by storing your own solar power. Trading with it, buying cheap power and using or selling it later, doesn't pay off yet.

Last updated 25 November 2026

The answer in three points
  1. Storing your own solar power (NOM): worth it. From 2027 each unit you store and use yourself saves about 26 cents.
  2. Trading: not worth it yet. A typical home earns about €90 to €200 a year. A battery costs around €5,000, so that takes 25 years or more to earn back, longer than the battery lasts.
  3. Selling stored grid power back: loses money from 2027, about 5 cents a unit, because you don't get the energy tax back.

Below, each point explained with a picture.

Why: two ways to use a home battery

1. Store your own solar power (NOM)

Around midday your solar panels often make more power than your home uses. The battery soaks up that spare power. In the evening, when you cook, watch TV or turn on the lights, the battery powers your home instead of the grid. So you hardly take anything from the grid, and hardly send anything back. That's why it's called "zero on the meter". Most batteries call this setting NOM or self-consumption.

Worth it from 2027 From 2027, solar power you send to the grid earns you only a few cents, but power you'd otherwise buy costs about 26 cents. Every unit the battery saves for you is worth about 26 cents.

2. Trading: buy cheap, use or sell expensive

With a dynamic contract, power is cheap at some hours and expensive at others. A trading battery charges from the grid in the cheap hours. In the expensive hours it either powers your home, or sells the power back to the grid. The battery's app usually does this for you automatically.

Doesn't pay yet Sounds clever, but three things eat the profit.

Why trading doesn't pay yet

  1. The price gap is small most of the year. After the power lost in charging, the difference between cheap and expensive hours is about 8 cents a unit on a normal day, and only about 2 cents in winter, exactly when you use the most power.
  2. The battery wears out a little every time. Like a phone battery, it can only be charged so many times. For a €5,000 battery, each unit it gives back "uses up" about 9 cents of the battery. That's about as much as you earn.
  3. The tax trap when you sell back. See the picture below.

The tax trap ("double energy tax")

Every unit of power you take from the grid includes energy tax: about 11 cents in 2027, more than the power itself. When you charge your battery from the grid, you pay that tax. When you sell that power back, you get the market price, but not the tax. The next household that uses your power pays energy tax on it again. The same unit is taxed twice. That's why people call it "double energy tax".

Until the end of 2026 you don't notice this, because the power you send back cancels out power you take (saldering). From 1 January 2027 that stops, so selling stored grid power back usually loses money.

This trap doesn't apply to using the stored power yourself. You pay the tax when you charge, but you don't buy that power again later, so it evens out.

Why "not yet"? What could change

  • Grid costs by time of day. Today you pay a fixed amount for the grid. From 2029, the regulator ACM plans to make the grid cheaper at quiet times and more expensive at busy times. A battery could then save on that too.
  • A fix for the tax trap. Parliament asked the government to come up with one, and it's being studied.
  • Cheaper batteries. If a battery costs half, the wear costs half too.

What to do

  • Judge a battery on storing your own solar power, not on trading. Ask the seller: "How much do I save without trading?" If it only pays back with trading, be careful.
  • No solar panels? Then a battery only has trading to earn from. That doesn't pay yet.

How we calculate this (the detailed numbers)

Example: a 10 kWh battery bought for €5,000, no solar panels, on a dynamic contract. The home uses about 4 units a day in the expensive evening hours (a terraced house using 2,800 units a year).

Gain = days × units per day × price gap after losses: about €0.08 on 245 normal days and €0.02 on 120 winter days (Energievergelijk, prices July 2025 to June 2026). Wear = €5,000 ÷ (6,000 cycles × 10 kWh × 90% round trip) ≈ €0.093 per unit delivered. The 6,000 cycles is a typical assumption, not a figure from a source. Tax trap: charge at €0.06 + 21% VAT + energy tax €0.0880 + VAT (€0.1065) ≈ €0.18; sell 0.9 units at €0.14 = €0.126; loss ≈ €0.053 per unit charged, before wear. Results rounded to €5.

Comparison sites reach the same conclusion: a 5 kWh battery without solar panels earns at most about €110 to €150 a year, and only if it hits the cheapest and most expensive hour every day. In a year-long test by Vereniging Eigen Huis, a few households earned up to €900 trading with a 20 kWh battery, but the researchers expect that to drop by about two-thirds as big grid batteries take the same price gaps. The energy consultancy DNV found that only trading on the grid's balancing market (through a provider's software) gave a positive return in 2025.
