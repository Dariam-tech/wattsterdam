Can a home battery make money trading on hourly prices? Not yet

The idea sounds great: charge your battery when power is cheap, use it or sell it when power is expensive. In the Netherlands, in 2026 and 2027, the numbers don't add up on their own. Here's why, with real figures.

Last updated 25 November 2026

Quick answer

Trading alone doesn't pay back a home battery yet. With a contract where the price changes every hour (a dynamic contract), a battery can charge in the cheapest hours and cover your use in the most expensive ones. After charging losses, that leaves about €0.08 per unit (kWh) on a normal day, and about €0.02 in winter.

Wear on the battery costs about as much. A €5,000 battery of 10 kWh that lasts 6,000 cycles costs about €0.09 per unit it delivers. A typical home earns roughly €90 to €200 a year from trading. That's a payback of 25 years or more, longer than the battery lasts.

Selling stored grid power back is worse. From 2027 you pay energy tax when you charge from the grid and get none of it back when you sell.

Two ways a battery "trades"

  • Charge cheap, use it yourself later. The battery fills up at night or on a sunny afternoon when prices are low, and powers your home in the expensive evening hours. You pay energy tax and VAT when you charge, and avoid them when you use the stored power instead of grid power, so the taxes cancel out. What's left is the price gap between the two hours, minus losses.
  • Charge cheap, sell back expensive. The battery sends stored power back to the grid when prices peak. Until the end of 2026, offsetting (saldering) hides the tax on this. From 2027 you pay energy tax and VAT on what you charge, and get only the market price for what you send back.

This is different from storing your own solar power for the evening. That is the main reason to buy a battery from 2027, and our house builder shows what it's worth for a typical home.

The second column needs a home that uses 9 units every evening, or selling the rest back to the grid, which loses money from 2027 (see below). Comparison sites come to the same conclusion: a 5 kWh battery without solar panels earns at most about €110 to €150 a year, and only if it hits the cheapest and the most expensive hour every single day.

Why it doesn't pay yet

  • The daily price gap is small most of the year. It's big on sunny spring and summer days, when midday prices drop, sometimes below zero. In winter, when you use the most power, cheap and expensive hours are close together.
  • You can only save on power you actually use. A home that uses 4 units in the evening can only replace 4 units, however big the battery is.
  • Every cycle wears the battery. Batteries come with a warranty of about 10 years or a set number of cycles. Cycling every day for a few cents a unit uses that up for very little.
  • Selling back is taxed twice from 2027. Example: you charge at €0.06 a unit before tax. With energy tax (€0.1065 including VAT in 2027) and VAT on the price, you pay about €0.18. You sell 0.9 units back at €0.15 and get €0.135. That's a loss of about €0.04 for every unit you charge, before wear. Parliament has asked the government for a fix for this "double energy tax". There isn't one yet.
  • Big batteries are taking the gaps. Large grid batteries trade the same price differences, so the gaps get smaller. In a year-long test by the homeowners' association Vereniging Eigen Huis, a few households earned up to €900 by trading with a 20 kWh battery. The researchers expect that income to drop by about two-thirds.

Where batteries do earn money

  • Storing your own solar power. From 2027, a unit you store and use yourself saves you about €0.26. The same unit sent back earns only a few cents. This is the battery's real job after the end of offsetting.
  • Balancing the grid, through a provider. Some battery providers let their software react to the grid's minute-by-minute balancing needs (the imbalance market). The energy consultancy DNV found this was the only earning model that gave a positive return in 2025. That income is under pressure too, and depends entirely on the provider's software and terms.

What could change: why "not yet"

  • Grid costs that depend on the time of day. Today households pay a fixed amount for the grid, whatever time they use it. The regulator ACM plans grid tariffs that depend on volume and time of day from 1 January 2029, with a final decision expected early 2027. A battery that avoids the expensive hours could then save on grid costs as well.
  • A fix for double energy tax on power that is stored and sent back. The government is looking at options.
  • Cheaper batteries. At half today's price, the wear cost per unit halves too.
  • More solar power on the grid means deeper price drops at midday, at least in summer.

What you can do

  • Judge a battery on your own solar power, not on trading. Ask the installer for the saving without trading income. If the payback is only short with trading, be careful.
  • Check the warranty: years and cycles, and whether trading counts against it.
  • Try it for your home in the house builder: add a battery and see the saving and payback. Have your yearly bill? Use the exact calculator.
  • Waiting is a real option. The rules that decide whether trading pays (grid tariffs, energy tax on storage) change in the next two to three years.

How we calculate this

Gain = days × units shifted per day × price gap after losses. We use about €0.08 per unit on 245 normal days and €0.02 on 120 winter days, from Energievergelijk's analysis of July 2025 to June 2026 prices. Wear = battery price ÷ (cycles × capacity × 90% round trip) = €5,000 ÷ (6,000 × 10 kWh × 0.9) ≈ €0.093 per unit delivered. The 6,000 cycles and €5,000 price are typical assumptions for a 10 kWh battery, within the €3,300 to €7,000 we use across the site. Energy tax 2027: €0.0880 per unit excluding VAT (Belastingplan 2027). Results rounded to €5.
