Terugleverkosten: what suppliers charge for your solar power

Your supplier pays you for the solar power you feed back, then charges you for feeding it back. From 2027 the only number that matters is the difference. Here's how it works, in plain English.

Last updated 14 November 2026

Quick answer

Terugleverkosten (feed-in costs) are what your energy supplier charges for the electricity your solar panels feed back into the grid. Most fixed and variable contracts have them. In early 2026 they averaged roughly €0.11 to €0.18 per kWh fed back, depending on the supplier and contract: a few hundred euros a year for a typical solar home.

The opposite is the terugleververgoeding (feed-in payment): what the supplier pays you per kWh. From 1 January 2027, when saldering ends, what you earn for each kWh you feed back is the payment minus the costs. Compare contracts on that net amount, not on either number alone.

Why suppliers charge them

Solar panels all produce at the same time: sunny middle of the day, mostly in summer. The supplier has to take that power when market prices are lowest, and it has to balance the grid when the sun comes and goes. The regulator ACM has investigated three times. In December 2025 it concluded that the feed-in costs suppliers charge are not unreasonable compared with what they actually pay, and that it doesn't expect them to rise further while market conditions stay the same.

The three ways suppliers charge them

MethodHow it worksGood to know
Per kWhA fixed amount for every kWh you feed back.Easiest to compare. The ACM wants every supplier to use this method.
Tiers (staffel)A fixed yearly amount per band of feed-in, for example one price for 2,000 to 2,250 kWh.Many tiered suppliers charge nothing below a threshold, from a handful of kWh up to about 1,000 kWh. Good for small systems.
Higher fixed feeNo separate line; solar customers pay a higher monthly fee.Easy to miss when comparing. From 2027 it still has to be shown per kWh on the invoice.

Most dynamic contracts, with hourly prices, have little or no feed-in costs, because the supplier passes the market price straight on to you. The ACM points out they only suit people who can live with prices that change every hour.

What changes on 1 January 2027

  • Saldering ends. Today each kWh you feed back cancels a kWh you take from the grid, worth your full rate of about €0.26. From 2027 you only get the feed-in payment, and pay the full rate for everything you take.
  • The invoice must show terugleverkosten per kWh fed back, whatever method the supplier uses. That makes contracts easier to compare.
  • The model contract (the ACM's standard contract every supplier must offer) has charged feed-in costs per kWh since 2026.

A worked example

A home feeds back 3,000 kWh in 2027. Two made-up contracts, to show why the net matters:

Contract AContract B
Feed-in payment€0.12 per kWh€0.06 per kWh
Feed-in costs€0.11 per kWh€0.00 per kWh
Net per kWh€0.01€0.06
You get for 3,000 kWh€30€180

Contract A advertises the higher payment but pays you €150 less. Compare the net figure, and compare it together with the price you pay per kWh you take from the grid: that part of the bill is usually much bigger.

What you can do

  • Use more of your own solar power when it's produced. Run the washing machine, dishwasher and EV charging in the middle of the day. Each kWh you use directly saves you the full rate of about €0.26; each kWh you feed back earns only the net payment.
  • Find your own numbers. Your jaarafrekening shows your teruglevering in kWh. Put it into the 2027 calculator to see what the end of saldering costs you.
  • Compare on net payment per kWh, at the amount you actually feed back. A tier threshold can make a big difference for a small system.
  • Look at a dynamic contract if you can shift your use to cheap hours and accept changing prices.
