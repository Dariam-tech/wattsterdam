Fixed, variable or dynamic energy contract?

Three contracts, three different bets on energy prices. Here's how each one works, what it costs to leave, what changes if you have solar panels, and why a "negative" electricity price almost never means free power.

Last updated 14 November 2026

Most Dutch households have a fixed contract: about 55% in August 2026, against 37% variable and 8% dynamic. The energy tax, the tax credit and the grid costs are the same whichever contract you choose; only the supplier's part differs. See how to read your Dutch energy bill.

Fixed contracts

You agree a price per kWh and per m³ for 1, 2 or 3 years. The supplier can't change it during that time. The exceptions are changes in the parts the supplier doesn't control: grid costs, energy tax and VAT.

Leaving early can cost an exit fee (opzegvergoeding). For contracts signed since 1 June 2023 it equals the supplier's actual loss:

exit fee = (your contract rate − the supplier's current rate for a similar fixed contract) × your remaining expected use

So if prices have risen since you signed, the fee can be zero. If they've fallen, it can be substantial. The supplier must tell you the amount and how it was calculated as soon as you cancel. Check that number before you switch.

Contracts running past 1 January 2027. If your contract doesn't yet say how the end of saldering will be handled, the supplier may only change prices or conditions if the contract allows it and you're told at least a month in advance. Otherwise you can cancel at no cost.

Variable contracts

No end date, and the supplier can change the price as described in the contract. Most change their variable prices twice a year, in January and July; more often is allowed if it's in the conditions and announced at least 30 days in advance. You can leave at any time, usually with one month's notice.

Every supplier must offer two model contracts set by the regulator, the ACM: a variable one whose prices change at most 4 times a year, and a 1-year fixed one. They're a fallback, not usually the cheapest option.

Dynamic contracts

You pay the wholesale market price, which changes every hour or, with some suppliers, every quarter of an hour since October 2025, plus a supplier markup. Prices are known a day ahead. Gas changes once a day. You need a smart meter. You still pay a monthly fee, energy tax and VAT.

What you pay for one kWh is roughly:

(market price + supplier markup) × 1.21 VAT + energy tax

With a markup of €0.025 and the 2027 energy tax of €0.1065 per kWh including VAT:

Market priceYou pay per kWhWhen this happens
€0.09€0.246A normal hour
€0.00€0.137Sunny or windy hours
−€0.05€0.076A "negative price" hour — still not free
−€0.12−€0.009Only now are you paid to use power

Why "negative prices" rarely mean free power: the energy tax is charged on every kWh you take, whatever the market price. With these numbers, the market price has to drop below about −€0.11 per kWh before you actually earn money by using electricity. That's why, by September 2025, there had already been more than 300 hours with a negative market price that year, but only about 5 in which electricity was really negative for consumers.

If you have solar panels

From 1 January 2027 saldering ends, which changes the comparison. On a fixed or variable contract you get a feed-in payment and pay feed-in costs; see terugleverkosten explained. On most dynamic contracts there are no feed-in costs, but what you feed back earns the market price of that hour.

That market price is lowest exactly when your panels produce most. Most negative-price hours fall between 11:00 and 16:00. During those hours a household with solar panels on a dynamic contract pays for the power it feeds back, unless the inverter can be switched off or limited when prices go negative. A dynamic contract works best for solar homes that can use their own power at midday (EV charging, heat pump boiler, washing) or have equipment that responds to prices automatically.

To see what the end of saldering costs you on a fixed or variable contract, use the 2027 calculator.

Which one fits you?

  • You want a predictable monthly amount: fixed. Compare 1-year and 3-year prices, and check the end date so you don't roll onto an expensive rate.
  • You expect to move, or want to switch whenever prices drop: variable. No exit fee.
  • You can run the washing machine, dishwasher, EV or heat pump when power is cheap, and can live with an expensive winter month: dynamic. It pays off most with an EV, a heat pump or a home battery that can be steered by price.
  • You have solar panels: compare the net feed-in payment on fixed and variable contracts with the market price on a dynamic one, and check whether your inverter can stop feeding back when prices are negative.
